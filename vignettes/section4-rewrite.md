## 4. The idea

You have built a matched design and the covariates look balanced. The honest next question is not "is it balanced?" but "is it balanced *enough* --- as good as what?" You need something to compare against, and the comparison has to name a standard you would actually trust.

Section 1 named that standard: a randomized experiment. A match is an attempt to build, after the fact, the experiment you could not run --- one that assigns treatment at random among units that resemble each other. So "is my match good enough?" means "does my design balance the covariates about as well as a real randomized experiment would?" The whole question turns on *which* experiment.

The obvious answer is the experiment that looks most like your design: re-randomize treatment within your realized matched sets, holding the sets exactly as you built them. That is the like-for-like reference --- your own sets, your own units, only the treatment labels reshuffled. It is also the reference section 2 showed to be broken. It measures imbalance against the spread of covariates *inside* your matched sets, and matching is the act of shrinking that spread. The better you match, the smaller the spread, and the more an unchanged residual difference stands out against it --- so a better match earns a worse verdict. The reference that is most like your design is precisely the one that collapses. That is the reason to look elsewhere: not perversity, but that the nearest mirror is the cracked one.

Here is the move. Re-randomizing within your matched sets is not the only experiment your design could be compared to --- it is the finest one. Picture a row of real block-randomized experiments, ordered by block size. At the fine end, tiny blocks of two or three nearly identical units: randomize within those and you get superb balance, because the units inside a block barely differ. At the coarse end, one giant block holding everyone: that is complete randomization of the whole sample, and it balances poorly, because it lets the large differences between dissimilar units fall wherever chance puts them. In between sit experiments with blocks of every intermediate size. These are not designs unlike yours. They are the family of experiments matching is trying to imitate, laid out from the best-balanced to the worst-balanced. The row is a ruler --- a measuring stick whose graduations are block sizes, each mark a known quality of balance --- and the question becomes: which mark on the ruler does *your* design's balance reach?

To read the ruler we need one number per experiment that says how your design's imbalance compares to that experiment's. We already have it. At any blocking, compute the design's percentile in the within-block re-randomization distribution of the same $d^2$ omnibus statistic used throughout: the fraction of within-block-shuffled assignments whose imbalance is at least as large as the one your design shows. A percentile near 1 means your design is more balanced than almost every random version of that experiment; a percentile near 0 means it is less balanced than almost all of them; a percentile of 1/2 means your design balances the covariates about as well as a coin-flip version of that experiment typically does --- it sits at the median, neither better nor worse than that experiment's ordinary luck.

Sweep the percentile across the whole row of experiments, from the matched sets (finest) up to the whole sample (coarsest), and plot it. I call the result the **resolution profile**. Its two ends are already familiar: at the finest blocking it reproduces the $d^2$ test, which collapses, and at the coarsest it reproduces the complete-randomization comparison. The information is in between, where the curve crosses from "worse than this experiment" to "as good as or better than this experiment."

The single number to read off the curve is that crossing. Define

    l* = the finest reference blocking at which the design's
         imbalance is no worse than the median block-randomized
         experiment at that blocking --- that is, the finest
         blocking at which the percentile first reaches 1/2.

In plain words: $l^*$ names the most demanding experiment your design balances the covariates as well as. Read it as a sentence about quality on a scale you already understand --- block size. A design whose $l^*$ is small balances the covariates as well as an experiment with small, tightly matched blocks: strong support on the measured covariates. A design whose $l^*$ is large only balances as well as an experiment with large, loose blocks: weak support. You have not compared your design to anything unlike it. You have placed its achieved balance on a ruler of real experiments and read off the finest one it matches.

Two honesties before we build it. First, $l^*$ is a reading of the curve, not a substitute for it; section 8 argues the curve $P(h)$ is the complete answer and $l^*$ is one summary of it, a somewhat noisy one (section 7). Second, $l^*$ speaks to the *measured* covariates only --- it says your design balances what you measured as well as experiment $l^*$ would, a statement of consistency rather than a certificate, and says nothing about what you did not measure (section 11).

One construction choice has to be settled before any of this is honest: how to coarsen. A block in a real block-randomized experiment holds units that resemble each other, so the ladder coarsens by merging the matched sets that are nearest on the matching score, and that rule is fixed in advance. This is not a free parameter to tune --- merging far-apart sets instead would flatter your design by a full rung (section 6). The reassurance on the other side: once the ladder coarsens by similarity, $l^*$ does not depend on which units you paired with which inside a set --- it reads off the treated and control covariate distributions sorted into resolution bands, which is what a skeptic who insists balance is about distributions, not pairings, would want it to read.
